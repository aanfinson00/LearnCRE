## Absorption (3)

**1. Pre-leasing a spec warehouse** — *industrial · intermediate · development*
A 400,000 SF speculative warehouse delivers in 6 months, 20% pre-leased. The submarket has 2.1M SF of vacant Class A, and net absorption has averaged 150,000 SF/quarter. Two other 300,000 SF spec buildings deliver the same year. **Q: Roughly when does your building stabilize at 90%?**
Best: Compute share-of-absorption — three buildings compete for ~600k SF/yr of demand, so a fair-share capture implies 24+ months, not the 6–9 a standalone read suggests. Wrong: divide 320k SF vacant by 150k/qtr (ignores competing supply); assume pre-lease % predicts the rest; "market is tight, it'll lease itself".

**2. Absorption vs. leasing velocity** — *office · beginner · asset management*
A broker cites "positive 80,000 SF net absorption this quarter" to support an office lease-up. Gross move-ins were 210,000 SF, move-outs 130,000 SF, and 60,000 SF of that came from one tenant consolidating into a new building. **Q: How should you read the number?**
Best: Net absorption is real but concentrated and partly a relocation inside the submarket — look at gross leasing, tenant count, and whether the consolidation backfills. Wrong: take it at face value; ignore it because net ≠ gross; treat it as a negative because of move-outs.

**3. Concessions masking slow absorption** — *multifamily · advanced · acquisitions*
Occupancy in a lease-up rose from 55% to 78% in 5 months, but face rents were cut 4% and 8 weeks of free rent were added. **Q: What does the lease-up pace tell you about demand?**
Best: Pace was bought with concessions — compare net effective rent and renewal behavior before extrapolating velocity. Wrong: velocity proves demand at pro forma rent; concessions are one-time so ignore; occupancy always lags rent.

## Comp selection (3)

**4. One trophy trade in the comp set** — *office · intermediate · acquisitions*
Eight sales comps range 6.6–7.4%; a ninth, a 5.1% trade, was an owner-user purchase of a headquarters building. **Q: What do you do with the 5.1% print?**
Best: Show it, but exclude it from the pricing range — owner-user buyers price on occupancy value, not investment yield. Wrong: average all nine; drop it silently; weight it highest because it's most recent.

**5. Comps across a rate move** — *any · intermediate · portfolio mgmt*
Your best-matched comps closed 9–14 months ago, before a 150 bps rise in the 10-year. Two weaker matches closed last month. **Q: How do you weight them?**
Best: Use the recent trades to set the direction/level of the market and adjust the better-matched older comps for rate movement. Wrong: use only the better-matched ones; use only the recent ones; ignore rates because cap rates are sticky.

**6. Price per SF vs. cap rate as the lead metric** — *industrial · beginner · acquisitions*
Subject is a 1990s cross-dock with 24' clear; comps are 2020-vintage 36' clear at 2× the $/SF. Cap rates are within 40 bps. **Q: Which metric should anchor your pricing?**
Best: Cap rate on normalized NOI, with $/SF as a replacement-cost sanity check — physical differences make $/SF non-comparable. Wrong: apply the comps' $/SF to your SF; average the two; use GRM.

## Lease economics (2)

**7. Free rent vs. lower face rent** — *office · intermediate · asset management*
A tenant offers a 10-year lease at $40/SF with 8 months free, or $38.50/SF with 2 months free. Landlord's discount rate is 8%. **Q: Which is better for the landlord, and what else matters?**
Best: Compare net effective rent and PV, then weigh mark-to-market on the sale: higher face rent supports valuation if the buyer capitalizes it, while lender/appraiser treatment of free rent can erase that. Wrong: pick the lower number of free-rent months; pick the higher face rent without PV; call them identical.

**8. Percentage rent breakpoint** — *retail · advanced · asset management*
A retail tenant pays $30/SF base plus 6% of sales over a natural breakpoint. Sales are $350/SF. **Q: What is the landlord's expected percentage rent, and what's the drawback?**
Best: Natural breakpoint = $30 / 6% = $500/SF, so none is due at $350/SF; percentage rent is an option the landlord holds, not income to underwrite. Wrong: 6% × $350 = $21/SF; 6% × $50 over; underwrite at 50% probability.

## Sensitivity (2)

**9. Which assumption moves value the most?** — *multifamily · beginner · acquisitions*
Same deal, three flexes: exit cap +25 bps, rent growth −1%/yr, OpEx growth +1%/yr over a 5-year hold. **Q: Rank them by impact on levered IRR.**
Best: Exit cap usually dominates, then rent growth, then OpEx growth — because the cap change hits the entire terminal value. Wrong: rent growth first; all equal; OpEx first because it compounds.

**10. Leverage amplifies the downside** — *any · intermediate · portfolio mgmt*
At 65% LTV, a 10% value decline costs ~28.6% of equity; at 40% LTV the same decline costs ~16.7%. **Q: What does this mean when sensitizing the exit?**
Best: Sensitize on equity, not value — leverage turns a modest value drop into an outsized equity loss and can breach covenants before the exit. Wrong: sensitize value only; assume debt is non-recourse so losses are capped; use lower leverage to improve returns in all cases.
