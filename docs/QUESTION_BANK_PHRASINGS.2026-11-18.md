## Absorption

### 1. Can this spec office building capture enough of the market?
*absorption · intermediate · office · roles: development, acquisitions* — fills: development

**Scenario.** You're underwriting a 200,000 SF speculative office development that is 30% pre-leased at delivery. The lender's model assumes stabilization at 90% leased within 24 months. Submarket net absorption has averaged 60,000 SF per year over the last five years, and two competing buildings totaling 400,000 SF deliver in the same window.

- Building size: 200,000 SF
- Pre-leased at delivery: 30% (60,000 SF)
- Stabilized occupancy target: 90% (180,000 SF)
- Submarket net absorption: 60,000 SF/yr
- Competing deliveries: 400,000 SF

**Q: What capture rate does the 24-month lease-up imply, and is it credible?**

- ✅ **100% of submarket absorption, which is not credible.** You need 120,000 SF in 24 months; the whole submarket absorbs 120,000 SF in that time, and 400,000 SF of competing space wants the same tenants. Underwrite 36–48 months, size the interest reserve and carry to match, and model concessions.
- ◻️ 24 months is fine because 60,000 SF/yr covers the 120,000 SF you need. *Treats the submarket's total absorption as if it all flows to you. Capture rate = your need ÷ market absorption; a new building rarely captures more than 25–40% even with a leasing edge.*
- ◻️ The 30% pre-lease proves demand, so 12 months is realistic. *Pre-leasing de-risks the first 60,000 SF, not the next 120,000. It says nothing about the pace of the remaining lease-up against new supply.*
- ◻️ Cut face rent 15% and the building will lease in 24 months regardless. *Price cuts shift share but don't create demand; with 600,000 SF delivering into 60,000 SF/yr of absorption, competitors match the cut and nobody's velocity improves.*

**Takeaway.** Lease-up timing is a capture-rate question: (SF you need ÷ months) vs (market absorption × a defensible share). If the implied share is above ~40%, the timeline is wrong, not the market.

- Capture rate = your required absorption ÷ submarket net absorption over the same window.
- Count competing deliveries as claims on the same absorption pool.
- A longer lease-up is a carry and reserve problem first; fix the reserve before the rent assumption.

### 2. How many months of supply is the industrial pipeline?
*absorption · beginner · industrial · roles: acquisitions, development* — fills: beginner, industrial

**Scenario.** An industrial submarket totals 50 million SF and is 4% vacant. 3.0 million SF is under construction and will deliver over the next 18 months. Trailing net absorption has run 2.5 million SF per year. The offering memo on your target asset assumes 3% rent growth starting in year 1.

- Inventory: 50.0M SF
- Vacancy: 4% (2.0M SF)
- Under construction: 3.0M SF
- Net absorption: 2.5M SF/yr (≈208k SF/mo)
- OM rent growth, year 1: 3%

**Q: Roughly how many months of supply does the submarket carry, and what does that do to the year-1 rent growth assumption?**

- ✅ **About 24 months; year-1 rent growth should be flat or near it.** Supply to absorb = vacant 2.0M + pipeline 3.0M = 5.0M SF. At 208k SF/mo that's 24 months. Two years of supply is a landlord-neutral to tenant-favorable market; expect concessions, not 3% growth, until the pipeline clears.
- ◻️ About 10 months; the pipeline is small relative to 50M SF of inventory. *Divides vacant space only (2.0M ÷ 208k ≈ 10). Months of supply must include what is under construction, since it competes for the same tenants.*
- ◻️ About 14 months; 3.0M SF delivering over 18 months is already absorbed on schedule. *Mixes delivery timing with absorption. The pipeline delivers whether or not it leases; the question is how long demand takes to fill it plus existing vacancy.*
- ◻️ 4% vacancy is tight, so 3% growth is conservative. *Reads today's vacancy and ignores the pipeline. Effective vacancy including the pipeline is (2.0 + 3.0) ÷ 53.0 ≈ 9.4%, which is not tight.*

**Takeaway.** Months of supply = (vacant SF + under construction SF) ÷ monthly net absorption. Under 12 months supports rent growth; over 18–24 months supports flat rents and concessions.

- Always add the construction pipeline to vacant stock before dividing.
- Compute effective vacancy on the enlarged base (inventory + pipeline) as a second check.
- Trailing absorption flatters a market where demand is slowing; haircut it if the last two quarters are below the average.

### 3. Is this lease-up ahead of plan or just buying velocity?
*absorption · intermediate · multifamily · roles: development, assetManagement* — fills: development

**Scenario.** A 300-unit multifamily development delivered three months ago. The business plan assumed 20 leases per month at $2,000 face rent with one month free on a 12-month lease. Actual leasing is running 25 per month, but the on-site team is offering two months free to get there. The sponsor's monthly report calls the lease-up "25% ahead of plan."

- Units: 300
- Plan velocity / concession: 20 per month · 1 month free
- Actual velocity / concession: 25 per month · 2 months free
- Face rent: $2,000/mo
- Lease term: 12 months

**Q: How should you read the "25% ahead of plan" claim?**

- ✅ **Ahead on velocity, behind on economics; net effective rent is about 9% below plan.** Plan NER: $2,000 × 11/12 = $1,833. Actual NER: $2,000 × 10/12 = $1,667. Faster leasing shortens the carry, but you've set a lower effective rent across a bigger share of the roll and taught the submarket that two months is the going concession. Track renewal rents in month 13 to see whether the discount sticks.
- ◻️ Ahead of plan; concessions are a one-time marketing cost and velocity is what matters. *Concessions on a 12-month lease are an 8.3% rent cut per month free, not a one-time cost. They also anchor renewal negotiations and comps for the next lease-up.*
- ◻️ Behind plan; two months free means the project is in trouble and rents should be cut to market. *Over-reacts. The spread is 9% on NER with faster absorption; the right move is to pull the concession back as occupancy climbs, not to cut face rent, which permanently lowers the roll.*
- ◻️ It can't be judged until stabilization. *You have the two numbers you need now: velocity vs plan and NER vs plan. Waiting a year to compare them wastes the window in which the concession can still be tightened.*

**Takeaway.** A lease-up has two scoreboards: units per month and net effective rent per unit. Report both. Velocity bought with concessions is a trade, not a win, until renewals prove the face rent holds.

- NER = face rent × (term − free months) ÷ term.
- One month free on a 12-month lease ≈ 8.3% effective discount.
- Watch month-13 renewal rents: that is where a lease-up concession either rolls off or becomes the market.

## Comp selection

### 4. Which rent comps actually tell you market rent for this strip center?
*comp-selection · intermediate · retail · roles: assetManagement, acquisitions* — fills: retail

**Scenario.** You're underwriting rollover on a 1990s unanchored strip center. The broker's rent comp sheet shows three asking rents at newly built centers nearby at $32/SF NNN. Your own canvassing turned up two signed leases at similar-vintage strips at $24/SF NNN on five-year terms with three months free. The broker's pro forma rolls every expiring suite to $32.

- Subject: 1990s unanchored strip
- Broker comps: 3 asking rents · new construction · $32 NNN
- Your comps: 2 signed leases · similar vintage · $24 NNN · 5-yr · 3 months free
- Pro forma rollover rent: $32 NNN

**Q: What market rent should you underwrite for the rollover?**

- ✅ **About $22.80 NNN effective, with $24 face.** Signed leases beat asking rents, and same-vintage product beats new construction. Effective rent on the real comps = $24 × 57/60 = $22.80. The $32 comps describe a different product at a price nobody has yet agreed to pay.
- ◻️ $32 NNN; it is what the market is quoting and the comps are closer. *Asking rents are a ceiling set by landlords, not a clearing price. Proximity does not fix a vintage and quality mismatch.*
- ◻️ Average all five at about $28.80. *Averaging a bad comp with a good one gives a half-bad number. Screen for relevance first (signed, similar vintage, similar anchor status), then average what survives.*
- ◻️ $24 face with no concession adjustment, since free rent is a one-time cost. *Three months free on a 60-month lease is a 5% rent reduction spread over the term. Ignoring it overstates rollover NOI and every value derived from it.*

**Takeaway.** Rank rent comps by evidence quality: signed lease > letter of intent > asking rent, and same vintage/anchor status > nearby. Then convert to effective rent before it touches the pro forma.

- Effective rent = face × (term − free months) ÷ term.
- An asking rent that has not transacted is an opinion, not a comp.
- Anchor status and vintage are bigger drivers of retail rent than distance.

### 5. What does an 88 RevPAR index mean if the comp set is wrong?
*comp-selection · intermediate · hotel · roles: assetManagement, acquisitions* — fills: hotel

**Scenario.** You're reviewing a 120-key suburban select-service hotel running $135 ADR. The broker's STR report shows a RevPAR index of 88 against a five-property comp set and pitches the gap as "upside to the set." The set includes two suburban select-service hotels, one 300-key full-service downtown hotel at $240 ADR, one extended-stay property, and one limited-service hotel near the airport.

- Subject: 120 keys · select-service · suburban · $135 ADR
- RevPAR index (broker set): 88
- Set members: 2 select-service · 1 full-service downtown · 1 extended-stay · 1 airport limited-service

**Q: How much should the 88 index drive your view of upside?**

- ✅ **Very little until the set is rebuilt; it is contaminated by product type and location.** A $240 ADR full-service downtown hotel drags the set's RevPAR up and the subject's index down for reasons the subject can never fix. Re-run the index against same chain-scale, same-location hotels; if it is still under 100, that is real upside.
- ◻️ Take the 88 at face value; STR data is third-party and the set was chosen by the broker. *Third-party data does not fix a first-party set selection. The index is only as meaningful as the comparability of the set.*
- ◻️ Drop the downtown hotel and keep the other four. *Better, but extended-stay and airport limited-service run different demand segments and rate structures. A two-hotel set is too thin; add same-scale suburban properties instead.*
- ◻️ Ignore the index and underwrite ADR growth to the market average. *Throws away a useful tool. A correctly built competitive set is the best evidence of whether the subject is under-earning its peers.*

**Takeaway.** A RevPAR index is a comp-selection exercise first and a performance metric second. Match chain scale, location type, and demand segment before reading the number.

- Index = subject RevPAR ÷ comp set RevPAR × 100; 100 is fair share.
- Same chain scale (economy, midscale, upscale) matters more than distance.
- Ask who built the set and when; brokers rebuild sets to produce an index below 100.

## Lease economics

### 6. Fixed 3% bumps or CPI with a 2%–4% collar: which does the landlord want?
*lease-econ · intermediate · industrial · roles: assetManagement, mortgageUw* — fills: industrial

**Scenario.** A credit tenant is negotiating a 10-year industrial lease at $8.00/SF NNN. The landlord has offered 3% fixed annual bumps. The tenant counters with CPI-indexed escalations, floored at 2% and capped at 4%. Your house inflation base case is 2% with a wide band.

- Term: 10 years
- Year-1 rent: $8.00/SF NNN
- Landlord proposal: 3% fixed
- Tenant proposal: CPI · 2% floor · 4% cap
- Inflation base case: 2%

**Q: Which structure should the landlord take in this base case?**

- ✅ **Fixed 3%; the collar makes the CPI deal a loser unless you expect inflation above 3%.** Year-10 rent at fixed 3% = $8.00 × 1.03⁹ ≈ $10.44. At CPI = 2% the floor binds and year-10 rent ≈ $9.56, about 8% lower. The 4% cap removes most of the inflation upside that would justify taking the CPI structure, and lenders and appraisers price fixed bumps with more certainty.
- ◻️ CPI; it protects real income and the floor guarantees 2%. *The floor protects you from deflation, not from under-earning. In the base case the floor is the whole story and you collect 2%, not 3%.*
- ◻️ CPI; a credit tenant on a long lease should get the structure it asks for. *Credit justifies a tighter cap rate, not a weaker escalation. Negotiate the structure on its economics and trade something else for credit.*
- ◻️ They're equivalent over 10 years, so take whichever closes faster. *Breakeven CPI equals the fixed rate, 3%, and the cap truncates the upside above 4%. The expected-value gap is roughly $0.90/SF in year 10 and compounds through the term.*

**Takeaway.** A CPI escalation with a floor and cap is a bet on inflation inside the collar. Compare the fixed rate to expected CPI; if fixed is higher, the floor is cold comfort and the cap is a giveaway.

- Breakeven CPI = the fixed bump being offered.
- A cap on CPI escalations hands the tenant the inflation upside the landlord was supposedly buying.
- Lenders underwrite fixed bumps at face; CPI bumps get haircut to the floor.

### 7. Why is the $30 renewal worth more than the $34 new lease?
*lease-econ · beginner · office · roles: assetManagement* — fills: beginner

**Scenario.** A 20,000 SF office tenant's lease expires in six months. The tenant will renew at $30/SF for five years with $10/SF of TI and a 3% commission. Alternatively, a broker has a new tenant willing to pay $34/SF for five years, but that deal needs $60/SF of TI, a 6% commission, and about nine months of downtime to build out. Your asset manager wants the higher rent.

- Renewal: $30/SF · 5 yr · $10/SF TI · 3% LC · no downtime
- New lease: $34/SF · 5 yr · $60/SF TI · 6% LC · 9 months downtime
- Space: 20,000 SF

**Q: Which deal puts more net cash in the landlord's pocket, and by how much?**

- ✅ **The renewal, by roughly $61/SF, about $1.2 million on 20,000 SF.** Renewal: $150 rent − $10 TI − $4.50 LC = $135.50/SF. New lease: $170 rent − $60 TI − $10.20 LC − $25.50 lost rent during downtime = $74.30/SF. The new tenant would need about $49–50/SF of rent to match the renewal.
- ◻️ The new lease; $4/SF more rent over five years is $20/SF more income. *Looks only at the top line. TI, commission, and downtime cost the new deal about $96/SF against the renewal's $14.50.*
- ◻️ The new lease; the higher face rent sets a better comp for the rest of the building. *The comp benefit is real but small relative to a $1.2 million gap, and buyers and appraisers adjust for TI and free rent when they read your roll.*
- ◻️ They're close enough that tenant credit should decide. *Credit is a tiebreaker when economics are within a few dollars per foot. Here the renewal wins by a wide margin before credit is considered.*

**Takeaway.** Net effective economics, not face rent, decide a lease. Every new deal carries TI, commission, and downtime that a renewal mostly avoids, so a renewal at a lower rent is often the better lease.

- Net lease value = total rent − TI − commissions − downtime rent.
- Downtime cost = months vacant × the rent you would have collected.
- Solve for the breakeven new-lease rent before you let a higher face rent win the argument.

## Sensitivity

### 8. How much does a 50 bps rate move hit cash-on-cash at 65% LTV?
*sensitivity · intermediate · multifamily · roles: acquisitions, mortgageUw*

**Scenario.** You're buying a stabilized multifamily asset for $50 million at a 5.50% cap, financed with a 65% LTV interest-only loan quoted at 5.50%. Rates move before you lock and the quote is now 6.00%. The deal team says it's "only 50 basis points."

- Price / cap: $50M · 5.50% (NOI $2.75M)
- Loan: 65% LTV · $32.5M · interest-only
- Rate: 5.50% → 6.00%
- Equity: $17.5M

**Q: What does the 50 bps move do to year-1 cash-on-cash?**

- ✅ **It falls from about 5.5% to about 4.6%, roughly a 17% cut in cash flow.** Debt service goes from $1.7875M to $1.95M, up $162,500. Cash flow drops from $962,500 to $800,000. On $17.5M of equity that is 5.50% → 4.57%. Leverage turns a 50 bps rate move into a 93 bps cash-on-cash move.
- ◻️ About 50 bps, from 5.5% to 5.0%. *Applies the rate change to the equity yield one-for-one. The rate applies to $32.5M of debt but the yield is measured on $17.5M of equity, so the effect is amplified by 32.5 ÷ 17.5 ≈ 1.9×.*
- ◻️ About 33 bps, since debt is 65% of the capital stack. *Measures the hit on total capitalization (0.50% × 65% = 32.5 bps of price), which is correct for unlevered cost of capital but not for the equity yield.*
- ◻️ Nothing, because the cap rate and NOI haven't changed. *Cash-on-cash is NOI less debt service over equity. NOI is unchanged, but debt service is not.*

**Takeaway.** Rate sensitivity on cash-on-cash scales with loan-to-equity, not loan-to-value. At 65% LTV every 50 bps of coupon costs about 93 bps of equity yield.

- Δ cash-on-cash ≈ Δ rate × (loan ÷ equity) on an interest-only loan.
- At 65% LTV, loan ÷ equity ≈ 1.86; at 75% LTV it is 3.0.
- Re-run the sensitivity before the rate lock, not after the deal team has anchored on the old yield.

### 9. Which assumption deserves the most diligence hours?
*sensitivity · advanced · mixed · roles: acquisitions, portfolioMgmt*

**Scenario.** A five-year hold shows $10 million of year-5 NOI and a 6.00% exit cap, for a $166.7 million exit. The model has four sensitivity toggles. Your team has one week of diligence left and wants to know where to spend it.

- Exit cap +50 bps: exit value −7.7% ($160.0M → $153.8M)
- Rent growth −100 bps/yr (3% → 2%): year-5 NOI −4.7%, plus lower annual cash flow
- Vacancy +200 bps (95% → 93%): NOI about −3.5%
- OpEx +5% (40% expense ratio): NOI −3.3%

**Q: Where should the remaining diligence time go?**

- ✅ **Exit cap first, but spend the time on the inputs that move it, not on the number itself.** It has the largest value swing per plausible move and is the least observable; you cannot diligence 2031's cap rate directly. Spend the week on what drives it: rollover and WALT at exit, submarket supply, and buyer depth for the asset class. Then test rent growth and exit cap together, since weak rent growth and wider caps arrive at the same time.
- ◻️ Rent growth; it compounds through every year and hits both cash flow and exit. *True, but rent growth is observable in the submarket today and already has evidence behind it. Diligence hours go where uncertainty is highest, not where the toggle is most elegant.*
- ◻️ Vacancy and opex; those are the ones you can actually verify in a week. *Verifiable is not the same as important. Both move NOI about 3–4% and are largely within operating control; confirm them, but do not spend the week there.*
- ◻️ Spread the time evenly across all four. *Even allocation ignores the ranking you just computed. The job is to concentrate on the one or two inputs that can break the deal.*

**Takeaway.** Rank sensitivities by impact × plausible range × how unobservable the input is. Exit cap usually wins on all three, and it should be stressed jointly with rent growth, not in isolation.

- Exit value change ≈ −(Δcap ÷ new cap); 50 bps on 6.00% is −7.7%.
- Inputs that are correlated in downturns (rent growth, vacancy, exit cap) must be stressed together.
- Diligence what you cannot look up: tenant intentions at exit, pipeline, buyer pool.

### 10. Which shock kills the development spread?
*sensitivity · intermediate · retail · roles: development, acquisitions* — fills: development, retail

**Scenario.** A ground-up retail development budgets $100 million all-in and underwrites $7.0 million of stabilized NOI, a 7.0% yield on cost against a 5.5% exit cap. The investment committee asks what happens if costs run 10% over, if rents come in 10% light, and if both happen.

- Budget: $100M
- Stabilized NOI: $7.0M (7.0% YOC)
- Exit cap: 5.5%
- Base spread: 150 bps
- Shocks: cost +10% · rent −10% · both

**Q: What do the three cases do to the spread, and what should the committee take from it?**

- ✅ **Each shock alone leaves roughly 80–90 bps; together they leave about 23 bps, and they tend to arrive together.** Cost +10%: $7.0M ÷ $110M = 6.36% (86 bps spread). Rent −10%: $6.3M ÷ $100M = 6.30% (80 bps). Both: $6.3M ÷ $110M = 5.73% (23 bps). Delays cause both overruns and softer leasing, so the joint case is the realistic downside. Require a wider starting spread or a hard contingency and lease-up reserve.
- ◻️ The cost overrun, because it hits the denominator and cannot be recovered. *Costs are the more controllable of the two and the single-shock math is nearly identical. The joint case is what breaks the deal.*
- ◻️ The rent shortfall, because it also lowers the exit value. *Correct that rent affects both YOC and exit, but on its own it still leaves 80 bps. Treating it in isolation understates the risk.*
- ◻️ Neither; 80-plus bps of spread is still accretive. *Only if the shocks are independent. A single late delivery produces both, and 23 bps of spread does not cover the risk of building.*

**Takeaway.** Development sensitivities must be run jointly. Cost and revenue shocks are correlated through schedule, and the spread that survives the joint case is the one you are really underwriting.

- Yield on cost = stabilized NOI ÷ total cost; spread = YOC − exit cap.
- A 10% cost overrun costs about 64 bps of YOC at a 7% base; a 10% rent miss costs about 70 bps.
- Set the minimum spread so the joint downside still clears 50–75 bps.
