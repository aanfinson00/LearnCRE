### 1. absorption · intermediate · multifamily · acquisitions/development — `leaseup-fair-share`
**Scenario:** A 240-unit new-build is entering lease-up in a submarket with net absorption of ~90 units/month. Three other lease-ups are open and competing for the same renters; the subject is comparable in quality and pricing.
**Data:** Units 240 · Submarket net absorption 90/mo · Competing lease-ups 3 (plus subject) · Stabilization target 95%
**Q:** What lease-up pace should you underwrite?
- ✅ A. ~22–30 units/month — a fair share of submarket demand across four lease-ups, so ~8–10 months to 95% (228 units).
- B. 90 units/month — the whole submarket's absorption, so ~2.5 months. *(Assumes subject captures all demand.)*
- C. 45 units/month — half of demand because the building is new. *(Newness is already shared by the other new-builds.)*
- D. Use the developer's pro forma pace; it's the sponsor's market knowledge. *(Sponsor pace is the input to test, not the evidence.)*
**Takeaway:** Submarket absorption is shared across competing lease-ups; underwrite a fair share, then haircut for concessions.

### 2. absorption · advanced · industrial · development/portfolioMgmt — `pipeline-months-of-supply`
**Scenario:** Industrial submarket vacancy is 4.0%. Trailing net absorption is 1.2M SF/year; 2.5M SF is under construction, 60% unpreleased. You're underwriting a 400k SF spec delivery.
**Data:** Vacancy 4.0% · Net absorption 1.2M SF/yr · Under construction 2.5M SF · Unpreleased 60% (1.5M SF)
**Q:** What does the pipeline imply?
- ✅ A. Unpreleased supply is ~1.25 years of absorption, so vacancy likely rises and lease-up for your building runs longer than the current 4% vacancy suggests.
- B. Nothing — 4% vacancy shows demand is strong. *(Backward-looking.)*
- C. All 2.5M SF delivers vacant, so 2 years of supply. *(Ignores the 40% preleased.)*
- D. Spec risk is the same in every market. *(Ignores pipeline/absorption ratio.)*
**Takeaway:** Compare unpreleased pipeline to absorption (months of supply), not just current vacancy.

### 3. absorption · intermediate · office · acquisitions/assetManagement — `sublease-hidden-vacancy`
**Scenario:** A broker touts +180k SF of positive net absorption and 12% direct vacancy in an office submarket. Sublease availability is 4.5% of inventory and rising; two large tenants are consolidating.
**Q:** How should you read the market?
- ✅ A. Total availability (direct + sublease ≈ 16.5%) is the real competitive supply; sublease space is usually discounted and competes with your direct lease-up.
- B. Use direct vacancy only; sublease is temporary. *(It competes at lower rents for the same tenants.)*
- C. Positive absorption means subleases will fill. *(Absorption can come from a few move-ins.)*
- D. Ignore both and use rent growth. *(Rent is a lagging output.)*
**Takeaway:** Add sublease to direct vacancy before judging how tight an office market is.

### 4. lease-econ · intermediate · office · acquisitions/assetManagement — `ner-two-offers`
**Scenario:** Two 10-year offers for the same suite. Offer 1: $30/SF face, 8 months free, $60/SF TI. Offer 2: $28/SF face, 4 months free, $40/SF TI. Flat rents, ignore time value and commissions.
**Q:** Which has the higher net effective rent?
- ✅ A. Offer 2 — $23.07 vs $22.00. (O1: 30×112/12=$280 − 60 = $220 ÷ 10 = $22.00. O2: 28×116/12=$270.67 − 40 = $230.67 ÷ 10 = $23.07.)
- B. Offer 1 — higher face rent. *(Face ignores concessions.)*
- C. Equal — same term. *(Concessions differ.)*
- D. Can't compare without a discount rate. *(Undiscounted NER is a valid first screen.)*
**Takeaway:** Compare on net effective rent; higher face rent can lose once free rent and TI are netted.

### 5. lease-econ · intermediate · retail · acquisitions/assetManagement — `percentage-rent-breakpoint`
**Scenario:** A retail tenant pays $30/SF base rent plus 6% of sales above a natural breakpoint. Reported sales are $400/SF.
**Q:** What percentage rent should you underwrite?
- ✅ A. $0 — natural breakpoint is $30 ÷ 6% = $500/SF, above current sales.
- B. $24/SF (6% × $400). *(Applies the rate to all sales.)*
- C. $6/SF (6% of sales above $300). *(Invented breakpoint.)*
- D. $30/SF — base rent doubles. *(Misreads structure.)*
**Takeaway:** Breakpoint = base rent ÷ percentage rate; no overage below it. Don't underwrite percentage rent you haven't cleared.

### 6. lease-econ · advanced · office · assetManagement/mortgageUw — `blended-rollover-assumptions`
**Scenario:** A suite rolls next year. Renewal probability 65%: renewal has 0 months downtime and $10/SF TI. New-tenant case: 9 months downtime, $50/SF TI.
**Q:** What blended downtime and TI do you model?
- ✅ A. ~3.2 months downtime (0.35×9) and ~$24/SF TI (0.65×10 + 0.35×50).
- B. 9 months and $50 — always assume the worst. *(Over-penalizes.)*
- C. 0 months and $10 — they'll probably renew. *(Ignores the 35% case.)*
- D. 4.5 months and $30 — simple average. *(Ignores probability.)*
**Takeaway:** Probability-weight renewal and new-lease outcomes rather than picking one.

### 7. comp-selection · intermediate · mixed · acquisitions — `sales-comp-quality`
**Scenario:** Four sales comps: (1) arm's-length single-asset 5.6% cap; (2) portfolio sale at 5.2%; (3) sale-leaseback to the tenant at 5.0%; (4) lender-driven distressed sale at 7.1%.
**Q:** How do you weight them?
- ✅ A. Anchor on #1; treat #2–#4 as bounding context with explicit adjustments (portfolio premium, leaseback structure, distress discount).
- B. Average all four (~5.7%). *(Mixes non-comparable transaction types.)*
- C. Use the lowest cap — it shows best pricing. *(Cherry-picking.)*
- D. Drop all but the distressed sale — it's the most recent. *(Distress isn't market.)*
**Takeaway:** Weight arm's-length comps; adjust or bracket the rest.

### 8. comp-selection · intermediate · office · acquisitions/assetManagement — `lease-comp-normalization`
**Scenario:** Three lease comps: A $34 face, 12 mo free, 5-yr; B $31 face, 4 mo free, 10-yr, signed 3 mo ago; C $36 face, 18 mo free, 15-yr, signed 2 yrs ago in a different class.
**Q:** What is the right approach to set market rent?
- ✅ A. Convert to net effective on a common term, weight the recent same-class comps (A, B), and treat C as outlier context.
- B. Average face rents ($33.67). *(Ignores concessions.)*
- C. Use the highest face rent. *(Biased.)*
- D. Use only the longest lease. *(Different class and stale.)*
**Takeaway:** Normalize for concessions and term; weight recency and class.

### 9. sensitivity · advanced · mixed · acquisitions/portfolioMgmt — `which-variable-to-stress`
**Scenario:** Base IRR 15%. ±50 bps exit cap moves IRR ∓2.1 pts; ±1% annual rent growth moves IRR ±0.9 pt; ±10% capex moves IRR ∓0.4 pt. Historically exit caps in this market have moved ±75 bps over a hold; rent growth ±1.5%.
**Q:** Where do you focus downside stress testing?
- ✅ A. Exit cap first, then rent growth — they have the largest IRR impact over the plausible range.
- B. Capex — it's the most controllable. *(Smallest impact.)*
- C. Rent growth only — operating variables matter most. *(Exit cap swing is ~3.2 pts vs ~1.35.)*
- D. All equally. *(Wastes attention.)*
**Takeaway:** Rank sensitivities by IRR impact × plausible range, not by controllability.

### 10. sensitivity · advanced · multifamily · development — `yoc-spread-cost-overrun`
**Scenario:** Total cost $50M, stabilized NOI $3.25M (6.5% yield on cost). Market exit cap 5.25%. Your threshold is a 100–150 bps development spread. Costs may overrun 10%.
**Q:** What does a 10% overrun do?
- ✅ A. YoC falls to ~5.9% ($3.25M ÷ $55M) — spread shrinks from ~125 bps to ~65 bps, below threshold.
- B. YoC unchanged — NOI is the same. *(Denominator rises.)*
- C. YoC falls to ~6.0% — still fine. *(Math and conclusion wrong.)*
- D. Overrun is covered by contingency, so ignore. *(Contingency is in the budget; this is beyond it.)*
**Takeaway:** Yield on cost is a thin-margin metric; a 10% overrun can cut the development spread roughly in half.
